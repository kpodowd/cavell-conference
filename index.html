Abstracts

Friday

"Something You Can’t Get At": Bergman, Cavell, and the Life of the Mind

Francey Russell (Barnard College and Columbia University)

This paper considers Cavell’s philosophy of psychoanalysis—the study of our inner “other mind”—through some issues in aesthetics. In a famous and enigmatic paragraph, Cavell writes that psychoanalysis is where “the human psyche as such, the idea that there is a life of the mind, hence a death, receives its proof. It receives its proof of its existence in the only form in which that psyche can (any longer) believe it, namely as essentially unknown to itself, say unconscious.” I elaborate the idea that the mind can receive and needs proof of itself as unknown to itself in connection with what I call aesthetic experiences of opacity. These are experiences of not understanding a work of art, yet where we find this to be satisfying and salutary rather than frustrating and disvaluable. While it can seem a truism that art is characterized by a dimension of palpable opacity and that this is part of why we care about it, it is not easy to account for this. I propose that aesthetic experiences of opacity are one way that the mind can receive “proof of itself as unknown to itself,” drawing on Cavell, Susan Sontag, and Ingmar Bergman.

The Avoidance of Marriage: Skepticism’s Aesthetics in The Drama (2026)

Benjamin Y. Goff (King’s College London)
Stanley Cavell's remarriage comedies depict skepticism not as an epistemological enigma to be resolved but as a modal condition to be recognized: the inherent impossibility of fully knowing another individual and the perpetual obligation to accept this unknowing. In Pursuits of Happiness, Cavell situates this dilemma within a framework of recurrent courtship: the couple's readiness to remarry repeatedly, fully aware of each other's opacity. Kristoffer Borgli's The Drama (2026) introduces an unforeseen addition to this tradition, exploring the genre's central inquiry (can one marry what is not fully knowable?) through a strikingly literalized skeptical crisis. Near their wedding day, Emma (Zendaya) reveals to Charlie (Robert Pattinson) that as a teenager she almost perpetrated a school shooting. This revelation is not merely a narrative complication but an artistic challenge: the film visualizes Charlie's effort to reconcile this disclosure through fragmented, nearly indeterminate flashbacks to Emma's adolescence; memory-images that are neither entirely his nor hers. This discourse proposes that the innovative formal aspect of The Drama lies in its depiction of the core structure of other-minds skepticism identified by Cavell: the inner life of the beloved becomes a picture that Charlie must assemble, perpetually uncertain whether he is perceiving or projecting. While Cavell's traditional remarriage comedies resolve skepticism through mutual acknowledgment and reaffirmed consent, The Drama interrogates whether such acknowledgment can endure when the revealed past is genuinely monstrous, and whether cinema's intrinsic image-making capacity, in depicting that past, might inadvertently betray or affirm the other's opacity. By pushing Cavell's paradigm to its most extreme limit, the film clarifies what remains at stake (though often concealed by comic tone) in the genre's gamble on love: acknowledgment is a moral risk rather than a metaphysical certainty. Consequently, the film advances Cavell's marriage-and-skepticism tradition into uncharted, increasingly challenging territory.

Democratic Defiance: With Stanley Cavell as a Guide

Alice Crary

This lecture enlists Cavell in thinking about and responding to democratic decline in the US and beyond. It opens with a partial diagnosis of the current constellation of overlapping threats to democracy, and it tells a compressed version of a tale about how a massive “springtime” for AI is being celebrated in the ideological terms of neo-liberal capitalism, the socio-economic system that the second Trump administration, with its disdain for democracy and fascist affinities, is intensifying in ways that reveal its underlying extractivist, colonialist, ethno-nationalist, and gender-essentialist colors. The crux of the confluence of forces driving this complex anti-democratic trend is a mechanistic conception of human endeavor that presupposes that, across employment and civic roles, it is replaceable without loss, and that any thought involved in these roles is reducible to efficiency-oriented calculations or computations. Cavell is a core ally for pro-democratic resistance because an insightful conception of language directly opposed to this conception is the heart of his wide-ranging oeuvre. It is pivotal for his inheritance from JL Austin and Wittgenstein, from the tradition of “ordinary language philosophy,” as well as for the inheritance from Thoreau and Emerson that Cavell takes to “underwrite” this tradition and illuminate its immense importance for democratic politics. In traversing these traditions, Cavell conveys a uniquely powerful account of what is at stake in losing a feel for challenges of thinking for oneself. Describing this loss, finding ways to vividly evoke we each face in refusing to go along with the crowd, is key to efforts to resist the technological, ideological, socio-economic, and political forces behind the sickening rush, in the US and beyond, to dismantle democratic institutions and build a “brave new world” for Capital to run amok and devour the Earth.

Cavell, Colonialism, and Mexican Cinema

Byron Davies (University of Murcia)

This presentation will offer some proposals for what would be required for a genuine encounter between Stanley Cavell and Latin American cinema, with attention to questions of separateness, acknowledgment, and taboo in both classic Mexican cinema and its later proxies, particularly director Arturo Ripstein. The focus will be on how much of a Cavellian reading of these traditions (mostly neglected by Cavellians) is intelligible and how much it genuinely is not, a question that turns on different framings of the connections between modernity and acknowledgement. For Cavell, certain modern and progressive (later transformed into “perfectionist”) myths of the twentieth-century U.S. were well-suited to Hollywood remarriage comedies, allowing mutual acknowledgement to be left just up to the couple. And yet in the Mexican films in question, the dialectic of incorporation and separateness central to them is inflected by what authors like Néstor García Canclini have analyzed as the uneven and hybrid process of modernity and modernization characteristic of colonialism, including U.S. neocolonialism, in Latin America. Thus, even when in 1991 Ripstein remakes the vital 1934 melodrama La mujer del puerto (The Woman of the Port, dir. Arcady Boytler and Raphael J. Sevilla) as a kind of remarriage comedy—with seemingly outrageous literalizations of Cavell’s notions of the screen as taboo-setting barrier and of acknowledgment as movement away from “incestuous privacy”— the meanings of “remarriage,” “taboo,” and “privacy” in this case are, and have to be, entirely different. We therefore need to get into view at once why seeming paradigms of Cavell’s genres in Mexican cinema employ only superficially similar tropes, and also how Cavell’s writing on what it is to derive some genres from others (here, a “kind” of remarriage comedy from a “kind” of melodrama of the unknown woman) genuinely deepens proposals, like film scholar Andrea Noble’s, about Ripstein’s “‘dismodern’ irony.” This presentation belongs to a larger agenda—with the hope of evoking something of the philosopher Enrique Dussel—of considering the consequences of treating colonialism and 1492, rather than principally Descartes and the New Science (as Cavell can be read as framing things), as foundational for modern skepticism about others.

Cavell, Skepticism, and the Lyric

V. Joshua Adams

The centrality of other-minds skepticism to Cavell's engagement with literature is well-known. This engagement concerns primarily drama and film: in "The Avoidance of Love," in the conclusion of The Claim of Reason, and in the additional essays in Disowning Knowledge, Cavell sees Shakespeare to have produced a tragic response to the problem, especially insofar as the skeptic treats "metaphysical finitude" as an "intellectual lack." Meanwhile, in Pursuits of Happiness, the so-called "Hollywood comedy of remarriage" gives us a comic alternative.

Though Cavell's contribution is singular and singularly important, it shares something with much other work in philosophy and literature in largely (though not completely) ignoring lyric poetry. This is especially ironic given that the situation of lyric, which tends to stress the interiority of the first-person, and their removal from the social world — Mill described it as akin to "the lament of a prisoner in a solitary cell, ourselves listening, unseen, in the next" — would seem to thrive on, if not produce, the skeptical problematic to which Cavell's work was addressed. Allen Grossman, for instance, suggests that the lyric produces "the discourse of the other mind par excellence." Why then did Cavell not write more about lyric poetry?

I suggest that lyric complicates our capacity to make the move from epistemology to ethics that marks Cavell's dissolution of the problem of other minds via acknowledgment. Though the situation of lyric makes acknowledgement harder, it does not make it impossible, and, with a glance at some other attempts to make acknowledgement into a form of reading, I will offer my own reading of a poem by Emily Dickinson as an example of what it would mean to read lyric poems in a Cavellian spirit.
